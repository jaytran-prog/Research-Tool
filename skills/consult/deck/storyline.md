# Storyline guide — insight first

The deck has one job: make the audience understand the answer, believe it, and know what to do on Monday. Everything here serves that.

## The non-negotiables
1. **Answer first.** Slide 2 (the executive summary) states the answer and the 3–4 insights that prove it. Nothing important waits until the end.
2. **Action titles.** Every title is a full-sentence conclusion with a "so what" — never a topic label.
   - ❌ "Competitor pricing" · ❌ "Results of the meta-analysis"
   - ✅ "Your real competitors are District 3 work cafés, not the four brands you named"
   - ✅ "Fasting and calorie counting lose the same weight after six months — so pick the one you'll keep"
   Titles are ≤ 2 lines (about 20 words), present tense, specific, and contain a number when one exists.
3. **Horizontal logic.** Reading only the titles, in order, tells the whole story. Test it before anything else.
4. **Vertical logic.** Each slide's exhibit proves its title — no more, no less. If the exhibit doesn't prove the title, change the title, not the exhibit.
5. **One message per slide.** If a slide needs "and" in its title, it's probably two slides.
6. **Insight over information.** An insight is a finding that is (a) surprising or non-obvious to this audience, and (b) changes what they should do. Rank candidate findings by surprise × decision impact and lead with the top ones. Facts everyone already knows go to the appendix or nowhere.
7. **Honesty is part of the design.** Every slide carries an evidence badge (strong / mixed / weak). Estimates are labelled. Disproved hypotheses are presented as findings ("We expected X; the evidence says Y") — they are often the most valuable insight.

## Storyline shape (SCR + pyramid)
- **Situation:** what the audience already agrees with (one line, no slide needed).
- **Complication:** why the obvious path is wrong or risky — this is where the surprising insight lives.
- **Resolution:** the recommendation and how to execute it.

## The standard skeleton (10–14 slides)
| # | Slide | Layout | Typical exhibit |
|---|-------|--------|-----------------|
| 1 | Cover: the question, client, date | `cover` | — |
| 2 | Executive summary: the answer + 3–4 numbered insights + the recommendation | `exec_summary` | — |
| 3 | Optional hero: the single number that carries the story | `hero` | hero stat |
| 4–7 | Insight slides, one per key finding, most important first | `standard` | bar, dot_ci, matrix_2x2, table, issue_tree |
| 8 | Options compared and why the recommendation wins | `standard` | options |
| 9 | The recommendation (what, why, conditions) | `standard` | bullets or table |
| 10 | Roadmap: phases, owners, decision gates | `full` | roadmap |
| 11 | KPIs and targets that decide go / no-go | `full` | kpi |
| 12 | Risks and what would change the answer | `standard` | table |
| 13 | What we know vs. what we're assuming | `full` | evidence_map |
| 14 | Sources | `sources` | — |

Add a `section` divider only for decks over 12 slides.

## Audience cuts
Tag each slide with `cuts` when it's not for everyone:
- **team** — operational: roadmap, KPIs, owners, risks in detail.
- **investor** — market, economics, risks, the ask; skip operational detail.
- **client** — the full story, presentable under the presenting firm's name.
Untagged slides appear in every cut. Every cut must still pass horizontal logic on its own.

## Ghost deck (before research)
After intake, write the storyline you *expect* to be true — as hypotheses:
- Each slide: hypothesis title (an action title marked as a hypothesis), the exhibit that would prove it, the data that exhibit needs, **what evidence would disprove it**, and the workstream that tests it.
- Include at least one hypothesis that challenges the client's own framing (e.g., "Your competitors may not be the ones you named").
- After research, every hypothesis ends as **confirmed**, **revised**, or **disproved** — and disproved ones usually become insight slides.

## Writing style
Plain words, short sentences, no jargon ("synergy", "leverage", "holistic"). Numbers with units and dates. Speaker notes carry the nuance the slide can't: caveats, the exact source wording, the question the audience is likely to ask.
